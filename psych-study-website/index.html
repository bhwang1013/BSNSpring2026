Definition & Core Symptoms

Anorexia Nervosa is characterized by severe restriction of food intake, intense fear of weight gain, and a distorted body image. Patients are significantly underweight but perceive themselves as fat. Two subtypes exist: restricting type (calorie restriction/exercise) and binge-purge type (restriction plus episodes of purging).

Bulimia Nervosa involves recurrent binge-eating episodes followed by compensatory purging behaviors (vomiting, laxatives, excessive exercise). Unlike anorexia, patients are typically normal weight, making it harder to detect.

Binge Eating Disorder (BED) involves recurrent binge eating with marked distress but NO compensatory purging. It is the most prevalent eating disorder and is strongly associated with obesity and emotional dysregulation.

Key Distinguishing Feature

  • Anorexia: severe low weight + distorted body image
  • Bulimia: binge → purge cycle, normal weight
  • BED: binge only, no purge, most common

Causes (Biopsychosocial)

  • Biological: Genetic predisposition; serotonin dysregulation affecting mood and appetite
  • Psychological: Perfectionism, low self-esteem, distorted cognitions about body and food; history of trauma
  • Sociocultural: Media idealization of thinness; cultural pressures; weight stigma

Treatment

  • Anorexia: Medical stabilization first (refeeding), then CBT and family-based therapy; SSRIs have limited efficacy
  • Bulimia: CBT is the gold standard; SSRIs (fluoxetine) reduce binge-purge frequency
  • BED: CBT + SSRIs or stimulant medications (lisdexamfetamine); address underlying emotional triggers

Nursing Relevance: Eating disorders carry the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder. Nurses must monitor vital signs, electrolytes (hypokalemia in purging), and provide non-judgmental therapeutic communication.

Definition

Schizophrenia is a severe, chronic psychotic disorder characterized by disturbances in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior that cause significant impairment in daily functioning.

Core Symptoms

  • Positive symptoms (excess/distortion of normal function): hallucinations (false perceptions — most commonly auditory), delusions (fixed false beliefs), disorganized thinking and speech
  • Negative symptoms (reduction of normal function): flat affect, alogia (reduced speech), avolition (lack of motivation), anhedonia

Phases

  • Prodromal: Social withdrawal, declining academic/work function, odd behavior — before psychosis emerges
  • Active: Full psychosis with prominent hallucinations and delusions
  • Residual: Psychosis subsides but negative symptoms persist; functioning remains impaired

Biopsychosocial Causes

  • Biological: Dopamine dysregulation (excess in mesolimbic pathway); structural brain abnormalities; strong genetic heritability (~80%)
  • Psychological: Cognitive distortions; stress vulnerability model (diathesis-stress); trauma history
  • Sociocultural: Urban upbringing; migration stress; expressed emotion in family (high criticism/hostility increases relapse)

Treatment

  • Antipsychotic medications (first-line for positive symptoms); atypical antipsychotics preferred due to fewer side effects
  • Psychosocial therapy: CBT for psychosis, social skills training, family psychoeducation
  • Combination is superior to either alone — meds reduce symptoms; therapy improves function and reduces relapse

Nursing Relevance: Nurses must monitor for medication adherence, extrapyramidal side effects, and metabolic syndrome. Therapeutic alliance is critical — avoid arguing about delusions.

Definition

Personality disorders are enduring, inflexible patterns of inner experience and behavior that deviate markedly from cultural expectations, causing significant distress or impairment. They are stable across time and situations.

The Three Clusters

  • Cluster A — Odd/Eccentric: Paranoid (pervasive distrust), Schizoid (social detachment, restricted emotion), Schizotypal (magical thinking, odd speech, social anxiety)
  • Cluster B — Dramatic/Emotional: Antisocial (disregard for others' rights, deceitfulness), Borderline (unstable relationships, self-image, impulsivity, fear of abandonment), Histrionic (excessive emotionality, attention-seeking), Narcissistic (grandiosity, lack of empathy)
  • Cluster C — Anxious/Fearful: Avoidant (social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy), Dependent (excessive need to be cared for), OCPD (preoccupation with orderliness, perfectionism — distinct from OCD)

Diagnostic Challenges

  • Overlap: Many disorders share features (e.g., BPD and Histrionic both involve emotional dysregulation), making differential diagnosis difficult
  • Subjectivity: Diagnosis relies heavily on clinician judgment; no biological markers exist
  • Bias: Research shows gender bias — women are over-diagnosed with BPD, men with ASPD for similar behaviors; cultural factors can be misread as pathology
  • Comorbidity: Most patients with one personality disorder meet criteria for at least one other

Treatment

Personality disorders are notoriously difficult to treat due to their ego-syntonic nature (patients don't see behavior as problematic). DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy) is the gold standard for BPD. Long-term psychotherapy is generally required.

Nursing Relevance: Understanding clusters helps predict patient behavior in clinical settings (e.g., BPD patients may idealize then devalue staff — "splitting"). Consistent, clear boundaries are essential.

Civil Commitment

Civil commitment allows the state to involuntarily hospitalize a person who has NOT committed a crime but poses a danger to themselves or others due to mental illness. Criteria typically require: (1) presence of a mental disorder, (2) dangerousness to self or others, and (3) inability to make safe decisions voluntarily. This reflects the state's parens patriae power (protecting those who cannot protect themselves).

Criminal Commitment

Criminal commitment occurs when a person commits a crime AND has a mental disorder that affected their ability to understand or control their behavior. It involves two key legal standards:

  • Competency to stand trial: Can the defendant understand the charges and assist in their defense? If not, they are committed for treatment until competent.
  • Insanity defense (NGRI — Not Guilty by Reason of Insanity): The defendant was so impaired at the time of the crime that they lacked criminal responsibility.

Insanity Defense — Key Facts

  • Used in fewer than 1% of criminal cases
  • Successful in only ~25% of those cases
  • Public greatly overestimates its use — a major misconception
  • Success leads to commitment, not freedom — often results in longer institutionalization than a prison sentence

Duty to Warn (Tarasoff)

Mental health professionals must break confidentiality and warn identifiable potential victims when a patient makes a credible, specific threat of violence. This legal duty arose from the 1976 Tarasoff v. Regents case.

Ethical Tensions

  • Individual liberty vs. public safety
  • Confidentiality vs. duty to protect third parties
  • Autonomy of the mentally ill vs. paternalistic intervention

Best Predictor of Violence: Past violent behavior — this is the single strongest predictor used in risk assessments, outweighing diagnosis alone.

Definition

Gender Dysphoria is a DSM-5 diagnosis characterized by significant distress or impairment resulting from the incongruence between a person's experienced/expressed gender and their assigned sex at birth. The diagnosis centers on the distress, not the identity itself.

Critical Distinction: Gender Dysphoria ≠ Transgender Identity

  • Being transgender (identifying with a gender different from assigned sex) is not a mental disorder
  • Gender Dysphoria is only diagnosed when the incongruence causes clinically significant distress or functional impairment
  • Many transgender individuals do not experience dysphoria — particularly after gender-affirming care
  • The DSM-5 retained the diagnosis to ensure access to medical care, not to pathologize identity

Biopsychosocial Causes

  • Biological: Prenatal hormone exposure; genetic factors; brain structure differences
  • Psychological: Identity development; internalized stigma worsens dysphoria
  • Sociocultural: Social rejection, discrimination, and family rejection significantly increase distress

Treatment

  • Gender-affirming care is the evidence-based standard: social transition, hormone therapy, surgical options
  • Psychotherapy to address co-occurring depression, anxiety, and trauma (not to change gender identity)
  • Family support is strongly associated with better outcomes
  • Conversion therapy is ineffective and harmful — condemned by all major medical organizations

Nursing Relevance: Use correct pronouns and chosen name. Create a non-judgmental environment. Recognize that distress often stems from social stigma, not from identity itself.

Overview

Sleep-wake disorders represent significant disruptions to the normal sleep cycle that cause clinically significant distress or impairment. They range from disorders of insufficient sleep (insomnia) to disorders of excessive sleep (narcolepsy).

Insomnia Disorder

  • Definition: Persistent difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, or early morning awakening, despite adequate opportunity for sleep — causing daytime impairment
  • Key feature: Performance anxiety about sleep creates a self-reinforcing cycle — the harder one tries to sleep, the more aroused they become (conditioned arousal)
  • Causes: Stress, hyperarousal, poor sleep hygiene, comorbid depression/anxiety; conditioned wakefulness
  • Treatment: CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia) is first-line — more effective than medication long-term. Sleep restriction, stimulus control, and relaxation training are core components. Sleep medications are second-line.

Narcolepsy

  • Definition: Neurological disorder characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness and sudden, irresistible sleep attacks regardless of circumstances
  • Cataplexy: Sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion (laughing, surprise) — NOT always present; its absence does not rule out narcolepsy
  • Other symptoms: Hypnagogic hallucinations (vivid hallucinations at sleep onset), sleep paralysis
  • Cause: Loss of hypocretin (orexin) neurons in the hypothalamus — often autoimmune in origin
  • Treatment: Stimulants (modafinil, amphetamines) for sleepiness; sodium oxybate for cataplexy; scheduled naps

Key Comparison

  • Insomnia = can't sleep when you want to
  • Narcolepsy = can't stay awake when you need to

Nursing Relevance: Both disorders affect safety (driving, operating equipment). Nurses must assess for sleep disorders in all patients, as they worsen virtually every other medical and psychiatric condition.
